Part 1 architectural assistant portfolio vs Part 2: what changes

  • Emerging Architects

Published 23 Oct 2026 · 9 min read

The short answer: a Part 1 architectural assistant portfolio is mostly academic work, and its job is to show that you can design and, above all, produce clear drawings. A Part 2 architectural assistant portfolio has a different job: to show that you can work on real projects with less supervision. So office experience moves forward, your role on each project becomes the headline, a resolved detail matters more, and your Part 1 work shrinks to a page or two at the back. This guide is for students and graduates applying for Part 1 and Part 2 roles in practice in the UK.

Written by blng studio, for architecture students, graduates and early-career architects.

Part 1 and Part 2: where the portfolios sit

"Part 1" and "Part 2" are shorthand for stages of the route to registering as an architect in the UK. The Architects Registration Board (ARB) describes the typical route, which takes a minimum of seven years, like this:

  • a three-year first degree, "often referred to as Part 1";
  • at least one year's practical experience;
  • two years' further study, "often referred to as Part 2";
  • at least one more year of practical experience;
  • a professional practice qualification, "often referred to as Part 3".

ARB requires at least 24 months' practical experience before registration, with conditions on when and where it's done (ARB, Practical Training Requirements). The RIBA's Professional Experience and Development Record (PEDR) splits it into two stages: Stage 1, "any time between the start of a Part 1 course and the start of a Part 2 course", and Stage 2, "from the start of a Part 2 course up until taking Part 3".

So there are usually two moments when you need a portfolio for practice: after Part 1, for your first year in an office, and during or after Part 2, for the experience that leads to Part 3. That's what this article compares.

The route is changing. ARB introduced new Competency Outcomes and Standards in 2024 and says it "will cease accrediting Part 1 qualifications from 1 January 2028". Existing Part 1, 2 and 3 qualifications stay accredited until they're replaced or until 31 December 2028. Practices will still use "Part 1" and "Part 2" in job titles for some time, but check ARB's website for where your course sits.

What each role is for

The portfolio follows from what the practice is hiring you to do, and the RIBA's guidance on professional experience is a useful guide to that.

Part 1 (Stage 1 experience). The RIBA's PEDR guidance says Stage 1 experience is "to determine if architecture is the right career; to apply some theory to practice". It also says that students "are unlikely to be given the opportunity to do a significant amount of design, particularly at stage 1". A practice hiring a Part 1 architectural assistant is mostly looking for someone who can draw, model and produce, and learn quickly.

Part 2 (Stage 2 experience). For Stage 2, the guidance is more demanding: "by the end of the Stage 2 experience, graduates ought to be able to run a small project with minimal supervision." A practice hiring a Part 2 is looking for evidence that you're on the way there.

The RIBA also says professional experience "is intended to be practical and technical, rather than purely creative". That's the thread through both portfolios: show you can design, but show that you can deliver too.

The Part 1 architectural assistant portfolio

If you're applying for your first year in practice, most of your work will be academic, and that's expected. What a practice wants to see, in our experience:

  • Clear drawings. At least one project with plans, sections and elevations that read as real drawings, at a sensible scale, not only diagrams and renders. This is the single most useful thing a Part 1 portfolio can show.
  • One strong design project. A project where the idea is clear and carried through, from the first diagram to the resolved drawings.
  • A sense of how things are built. A construction or technical study, a detail, a structural model, or a project where you thought about materials. It doesn't need to be complete; it needs to show interest.
  • Range, briefly. A second project of a different scale or type, and a page of sketches, models or making.
  • Any work experience, even short, with your role stated.

Keep software skills in the drawings rather than in a list. Our architecture portfolio tips cover the first minute, the opening pages and showing your role.

The Part 2 architectural assistant portfolio

By the time you apply for Part 2 roles, you'll usually have at least a year in practice behind you and a different kind of work in your portfolio. The changes we'd expect to see:

  • Office work moves forward. What you did in practice is often more relevant to the reviewer than your thesis. Put it first or second, depending on the role.
  • Your role becomes the headline. Say what you did on each office project, and at which stages. Using the RIBA Plan of Work stages is a clear way to do it: for example, "Stage 2 to 4: planning drawings, façade study, coordination with the structural engineer".
  • A detail you resolved. A drawn and annotated detail shows the technical side the RIBA expects Stage 2 experience to build.
  • Part 2 design work shows how your thinking has developed. Your strongest Part 2 project can still lead, especially for a design-led practice. A second one can be condensed.
  • Part 1 work shrinks. One or two pages at the back, or out altogether if it no longer represents you.

If you're between the two. Applying during your Part 2 course, with a year out behind you but little Part 2 work yet? Lead with whichever is stronger and more relevant to the role, your office work or your best Part 1 project, and say clearly where you are in your studies. A practice reading it needs to know which kind of role you're applying for.

Check what you can share. Office work belongs to the practice and its clients. Ask your employer what you can include, and show it only in the way they agree.

What changes, side by side

Part 1 portfolio Part 2 portfolio
Main evidence Academic design work Office work and Part 2 design work
What it needs to prove You can design and produce clear drawings You can deliver real projects with less supervision
Lead project Your strongest academic project Your strongest Part 2 project or office project
Your role Stated on any group work The headline on every office project, by RIBA stage
Technical work A construction study shows interest A resolved detail shows ability
Earlier work First- and second-year work, briefly Part 1 work in one or two pages

Notice what doesn't change. Both portfolios need clear drawings, a clear idea in each project, and an honest account of what you did. A Part 2 portfolio doesn't stop being about design; it adds evidence that you can deliver it. And both are read the same way, quickly and often on a screen, so the first pages carry most of the weight.

The structure underneath both stays the same: a short front, your projects in order of strength, other work and contact at the back. We cover that skeleton in How to structure an architecture portfolio.

Common mistakes when moving from Part 1 to Part 2

  • Adding the office work at the back of the old Part 1 portfolio, instead of rebuilding the order.
  • Leaving out your role on office projects, so the reviewer can't tell whether you drew the plans or attended the meetings.
  • Keeping every Part 1 project. If your Part 2 work is stronger, your Part 1 work doesn't need full sections.
  • Office work with no story. A set of planning drawings without a line about the brief, the constraint and what you did reads as a print-out.
  • The same portfolio for every practice. A design-led studio and a delivery-focused practice will read the same pages differently. Reorder the lead projects for each.
  • Showing only the polished images from practice, and none of the production work: the drawings, details and coordination that show what you can do.

Frequently asked questions

What should a Part 1 architectural assistant portfolio include?

Usually one or two strong academic projects with clear plans, sections and elevations, a construction or technical study, a page of sketches or models, and any work experience with your role stated.

How is a Part 2 architecture portfolio different from a Part 1 one?

It brings office experience forward, makes your role on each project the headline, shows a resolved detail, and cuts Part 1 work down to a page or two. The aim shifts from "I can design and draw" to "I can work on real projects with less supervision".

Should I include office work in a Part 2 portfolio?

Yes, usually first or second, with your role and the RIBA stages you worked on. Ask your employer what you're allowed to share.

Should a Part 1 portfolio include work experience?

Yes, if you have any, even a short placement. Keep it brief, say what you did and for how long, and put it after your strongest academic projects unless it's directly relevant to the role.

Is Part 1 being phased out?

ARB says it "will cease accrediting Part 1 qualifications from 1 January 2028", and existing Part 1, 2 and 3 qualifications remain accredited until superseded or 31 December 2028. Check ARB's website for what this means for your course.

How long is Part 1 and Part 2?

On ARB's typical route, Part 1 is a three-year first degree and Part 2 is two years' further study, with at least a year of practical experience after each. The whole route takes at least seven years.
